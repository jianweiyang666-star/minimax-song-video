# Songwriting and style brief

Read this reference when analyzing a reference track, writing new lyrics, or revising lyrics that feel generic, awkward, or unsingable.

## Analyze the reference without copying it

Use the supplied audio whenever available. Capture:

- tempo and perceived tempo, meter, backbeat, subdivision density, and whether the vocal sits ahead of or behind the beat;
- average lyric phrase length, rests, pickups, long vowels, and how many lines form one melodic thought;
- verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, and outro durations;
- bass, drums, keys, guitars, synths, ambience, and how the arrangement changes by section;
- vocal register, brightness, breath, nasality, microphone distance, diction, and emotional restraint;
- energy curve, harmonic color, overall duration, and explicit negative constraints.

Translate those findings into abstract production language. Do not ask MiniMax to reproduce a released melody, lyric, or real artist's identity.

## Lyric creation order

1. Reduce the story to one emotional contradiction, such as “still familiar, no longer close.”
2. Choose narrator, time, place, and two or three recurring concrete objects.
3. Write the chorus hook first. It should be speakable in ordinary conversation and carry the title.
4. Set a rhyme map. A useful default is three or four related line endings, then a deliberate rhyme change.
5. Match line lengths to the target groove and reference phrase lengths before polishing individual words.
6. Draft verse and pre-chorus details that earn the hook; do not repeat the premise in every section.
7. Read every line aloud and remove wording that exists only to force a rhyme.
8. Run a human-writing pass: replace abstract summaries with observable actions, cut symmetrical filler, and allow a few natural irregularities.

Prefer internal rhyme, vowel family, and repeated consonants over identical end words. Preserve already-good lines when revising; change only weak or awkward lines unless the user requests a rewrite.

## Language rules

### Chinese song

- Sung lyric lines contain natural Chinese only.
- Avoid English catchphrases, English ad-libs, romanized fillers, and translated duplicate lines.
- Use everyday syntax, concrete images, and punctuation that suggests breathing rather than formal prose.
- An English translation, if requested, is a separate subtitle artifact and is never pasted into the music-generation lyrics field.

### English song

- Sung lyric lines contain English only.
- Write for stress pattern and vowel sustain, not Chinese word order translated literally.
- Produce a separate natural Chinese subtitle translation. Translate meaning and emotional register rather than matching English syntax word for word.

## Final lyric review

Check:

- the hook is memorable after one listen;
- adjacent lines are singable at the target tempo;
- rhyme supports meaning rather than replacing it;
- verse details change or deepen the situation;
- repeated choruses are intentional and included in full;
- no translation or production instruction appears among sung lines.
